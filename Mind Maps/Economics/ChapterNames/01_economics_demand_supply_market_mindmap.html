Branches of Economics

1. Microeconomics

  • Study of individual units: individual firm, individual industry, individual market
  • Components: Theory of Demand, Theory of Production, Price Determination, Factor Pricing/Distribution

2. Macroeconomics

  • Study of the overall economy of a nation or region
  • Components: National output/income, employment, unemployment, inflation, growth, fiscal and monetary policy
⚠ EXAM TRAP
Microeconomics = individual unit level; Macroeconomics = aggregate/national level. Questions often swap "firm" and "national income" between the two to test this distinction.
Law of Demand

Effective demand = Desire + Willingness + Ability to buy a quantity at a given price and time

Own price ↓ → Quantity demanded ↑ | Own price ↑ → Quantity demanded ↓ (other factors constant)

Own price and quantity demanded are inversely related, ceteris paribus; an own-price change causes movement along the same demand curve.

Demand Curve
  • Slope of Demand Curve: Generally downward (negative slope)
  • Own price ↑ → Quantity demanded ↓; own price ↓ → Quantity demanded ↑ (movement along the curve)

Example: When price is ₹1, quantity demanded is 3 units. When price is ₹3, quantity demanded is 1 unit.

Market Equilibrium
Quantity demanded = Quantity supplied → Neither shortage nor surplus
⚡ QUICK RECALL
At equilibrium, quantity demanded exactly equals quantity supplied — no shortage, no surplus.
Exceptions to the Law of Demand

Giffen Goods

  • Strongly inferior goods for which the adverse real-income effect outweighs the substitution effect; quantity demanded can rise with own price
  • Own price ↑ → Quantity demanded ↑: movement along an upward-sloping demand curve; the curve does not shift merely because own price changes

Prestige / Conspicuous-Consumption Goods (Veblen Goods)

  • Luxury watches, designer handbags and high-end status cars — higher prices can enhance prestige or exclusivity
⚠ EXAM TRAP
Giffen Goods and Veblen Goods both defy the Law of Demand, but for different reasons — Giffen (necessity/income effect) vs Veblen (status/prestige). Frequently swapped in options.

Elasticity of Demand — Overview

Elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to changes in own price, income or the price of a related good.

  • Income Elasticity of Demand
  • Cross Elasticity of Demand
  • Price Elasticity of Demand
1. Income Elasticity of Demand (Ey)

Measures change in quantity demanded of a commodity with respect to changes in buyer's income.

Ey = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income
2. Cross Elasticity of Demand (Ec)

Measures the percentage change in quantity demanded of one good (A) in response to a percentage change in price of another related good (B).

Ec = % change in quantity demanded of A ÷ % change in price of B
3. Price Elasticity of Demand (Ep)

Measures changes in quantity demanded of a commodity with respect to changes in its own price.

Ep = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in own price

Types of Price Elasticity of Demand

TypeDescription
Perfectly Elastic DemandHorizontal curve; infinite elasticity in the idealised case
Perfectly Inelastic DemandVertical curve; quantity demanded is unchanged; elasticity = 0
Unitary Elastic DemandEqual absolute percentage changes in own price and quantity demanded; |Ep| = 1
Relatively Elastic DemandPercentage change in quantity demanded exceeds percentage change in own price in absolute value; |Ep| > 1
Relatively Inelastic DemandPercentage change in quantity demanded is smaller than percentage change in own price in absolute value; |Ep| < 1
⚠ EXAM TRAP
Perfectly elastic = horizontal curve (infinite elasticity); perfectly inelastic = vertical curve (zero elasticity). For ordinary demand, Ep is negative and is often reported in absolute value.
Law of Supply

Supply: Quantities producers are willing and able to offer at various prices during a given period, other things equal.

  • Law of Supply: Own price and quantity supplied move in the same direction, ceteris paribus; an own-price change causes movement along the supply curve
  • Higher own price → Higher quantity supplied
  • Lower own price → Lower quantity supplied
Supply Curve
  • Slope of Supply Curve: Generally upward
  • Price of goods has a positive relationship with quantity supplied

More money supply does not guarantee an equal increase in real output: effects depend on prices, interest rates, expectations and capacity. A higher market interest rate tends to reduce desired money balances by raising the opportunity cost of holding money.

⚡ QUICK RECALL
Demand curve slopes downward; Supply curve slopes upward — their intersection point gives market equilibrium.
Forms of Market
Market FormKey Features
Perfect CompetitionLarge number of buyers and sellers · Free entry and exit of firms · Homogeneous goods · Same price · Price determined by demand and supply (market forces)
MonopolyOnly one seller · No close substitutes · Restrictions on entry of firms · Price maker constrained by market demand · Demand curve less elastic than monopolistic competition
Monopolistic CompetitionMany buyers and sellers · Seller's product differs from others · Product differentiation (colour, design, size, etc.)
OligopolyLimited number of firms · Large number of buyers · Barriers to entry (not easy) · Price/output decisions of one firm affect others
⚠ EXAM TRAP
Monopoly = one seller, a price maker constrained by market demand. Oligopoly = a few dominant firms with strategically interdependent decisions.
