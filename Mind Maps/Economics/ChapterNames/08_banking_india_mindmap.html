Development of Banking in India
  • First Bank in India: Bank of Hindustan, Calcutta (1770) — on European Banking pattern
  • Three Presidency Banks: Bank of Calcutta (1806; renamed Bank of Bengal, 1809), Bank of Bombay (1840), Bank of Madras (1843)
  • Combining all three → Imperial Bank of India (1921)
  • Nationalised on 1st July 1955, renamed State Bank of India (SBI)
  • Early Indian-managed joint-stock commercial bank: Oudh (Awadh) Commercial Bank (1881)
  • First Swadeshi Bank, purely managed by Indians with Indian capital: PNB (incorporated 1894; operations began 1895)
⚠ EXAM TRAP
Oudh Commercial Bank (1881) was an early Indian-managed joint-stock bank. PNB describes itself as the first Swadeshi Bank; incorporation was in 1894 and business began in 1895.

The 2020 mega-merger reduced PSBs significantly — remember which bank absorbed which (PNB absorbed OBC+UBI; Canara absorbed Syndicate; Union absorbed Andhra+Corporation; Indian Bank absorbed Allahabad).

Co-operative Banks
  • State Co-operative Bank
  • District / Central Co-operative Bank
  • Primary Credit Societies

State Co-operative Banks (SCBs)

Provide loans to Central/District Co-operative Banks.

Central Co-operative Bank

Area of work limited to one district; acts as a bridge between State Co-operative Banks and Co-operative Credit Societies.

Primary Credit Societies

Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS): village-level institutions providing short- and medium-term credit and related services to members, especially farmers.

Urban Co-operative Banks (UCB)

Scope limited to metros, semi-urban, urban centres; provides loans to micro/small/medium industries, small entrepreneurs, retail traders. UCB totals change over time and require a dated RBI count.

⚡ QUICK RECALL
Lead Bank Scheme (1969): Gadgil Study Group proposed an area approach; Nariman Committee endorsed the scheme. Lead banks coordinate district-level banking and credit plans with other banks/agencies.

Multi-State Co-operative Society: Registered under the Multi-State Co-operative Societies Act, 2002.

Functions of RBI
  • Regulation and control of the banking system (Banking Regulation Act, 1949)
  • RBI issues banknotes except the ₹1 note (signed by Finance Secretary); Government of India issues coins and RBI circulates them as its agent
  • Regulate the banking and financial system
  • Lead the government in institutions like IMF, World Bank
  • Working as banker to the government
  • Controlling credit
  • Act as a bank of banks
  • Conservation of foreign exchange reserves
  • Regulation of foreign exchange rates
  • Conduct of monetary policy
  • Developmental work
  • Compilation and publication of data

Minimum Reserve System: at least ₹200 crore reserve, with at least ₹115 crore in gold and balance in foreign securities. Clean/clear-note policy maintains clean notes and withdraws unfit notes.

Monetary Policy & MPC

Monetary Policy is formulated by RBI in consultation with the MPC; works to control money and credit in the economy; target is price stability in the economy.

Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

Formed On Recommendation OfUrjit Patel Committee, 24 September 2016
ChairmanGovernor of RBI
Members6 total: Governor (Chairperson), monetary-policy Deputy Governor, RBI officer nominated by Central Board, and 3 external members appointed by Central Government
Quantitative Monetary Instruments
ToolKey Facts
Bank RateReference rate aligned with MSF; 5.50% as of 6 August 2026. Monetary tightening generally involves raising policy rates
Repo RateCollateralised short-term RBI liquidity; 5.25% as of 6 August 2026. Rate increases tend to tighten financial conditions
Reverse Repo RateLegacy fixed-rate absorption facility; 3.35% as of 6 August 2026. SDF is the main standing absorption facility
SLRRetention of NDTL by banks (min 0%, max 40%); minimum limit abolished in 2007; currently 18%; increase → decreases liquidity
CRRShare of NDTL kept as cash with RBI: 3.00% as of 6 August 2026; old 3–15% statutory range is no longer valid
Open Market Operations (OMO)RBI buys/sells securities; selling absorbs excess liquidity, buying injects liquidity
Marginal Standing FacilityStanding overnight RBI borrowing facility for eligible banks against eligible securities, normally above the repo rate
Standing Deposit FacilityEffective 8 April 2022; RBI absorbs liquidity without collateral. 5.00% on 6 August 2026, 25 basis points below repo
Liquidity Management Framework (LMF)Keeps short-term money-market rates aligned with policy via repo, SDF, MSF, variable-rate operations and OMOs; no timeless 1% NDTL/day rule
Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF)Current corridor: repo injection, SDF standing absorption, MSF upper lending facility; reverse repo is a legacy absorption tool
Base RateIntroduced 1 July 2010; later superseded for new lending by MCLR and external benchmarks for specified loans
External Benchmark Based Lending Rate (EBLR)External-benchmark-linked pricing mandated from 1 October 2019 for specified new floating-rate retail/personal and MSME loans
⚠ EXAM TRAP
Repo Rate (RBI lends to banks) vs Reverse Repo Rate (banks lend/deposit with RBI) are opposite directions of the same tool — commonly reversed in questions.
Selective / Qualitative Instruments

  • Purpose: Prevent gambling/hoarding; prevent unreasonable price increases

Basel Norms

Basel is a Swiss city. G10 central-bank Governors formed the Basel Committee at the end of 1974; BIS was established in 1930. Basel norms strengthen regulation, supervision, risk management and capital standards internationally.

NormsDetails
Basel-IIssued 1988; India introduced risk-weighted capital-adequacy norms from 1992 in phases
Basel-IIReleased in 2004
Basel-IIICore standards from 2010; India implemented Basel III from 1 April 2013. Regulatory capital: Tier 1 and Tier 2; Tier 3 abolished

Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR), also known as Capital-to-Risk Weighted Assets Ratio (CRAR): measurement of a bank's available capital as a % of risk-weighted credit exposure.

Non-Performing Assets (NPA)

NPA classification depends on specified criteria; for term loans, interest and/or principal overdue for more than 90 days is the standard trigger.

Categories

  • Sub-standard Assets
  • Doubtful Assets
  • Loss Assets

Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFC)

Companies incorporated under the Companies Act and conducting permitted financial business (lending, investment, leasing and other services); RBI registration generally required under Section 45-IA unless exempt.

TypeKey Facts
NBFC-DAccepts deposits; requires registration with RBI; demand deposit not accepted
NBFC-NDDoes not accept public deposits; RBI registration generally required unless specifically exempt

NBFCs need not maintain SLR/CRR; do not form part of the payment and settlement system; no insurance facility by DICGC for depositors.
